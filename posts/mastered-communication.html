Mastered communication? Then you haven't understood it

If someone tells me they have mastered communication, I quietly suspect they have not yet understood it. Not because they lack skill, but because the very claim overlooks what makes communication difficult — and worthwhile.

Here is what we are up against. We humans live through experiences that sound similar when described: a loss, a celebration, a long day at work. Yet each of us inhabits those events alone. No two interiors are the same. Language is the bridge we have built across that distance — a way to carry ideas from one private world into another. But we often read the other person's words through our own frame of reference, not through the narrator's. That gap can be narrowed. It can never be closed.

So where does the confusion begin? I suspect it starts with a substitution. We reduce communication to confidence in a room, or to the skill of public speaking. Both can draw on good communication, but neither defines it. A person can be supremely self-assured and still say nothing of value — or nothing that reaches the other side.

What is it, then? Effective communication is not explaining your own understanding. It is explaining at the level of the person in front of you, in service of their comprehension rather than your own fluency. It is less a talent than a set of principles practiced until they become a habit. The same idea, explained to two different people, calls for two different explanations — not because the idea has changed, but because the listener has.

In the posts that follow, we will work through the ordinary mistakes and hidden assumptions we make every day. The ones most of us never notice we are making.

I Active Listening

Why place this first? Because communication is an exchange of information, not a performance. Active listening is not waiting for your turn to speak. It is listening to understand — not only the content the other person is saying, but the level at which they are operating: the context that shaped their understanding, the edges where their familiarity runs out. That is the level at which you have to meet them.

Calibrating your language to the person in front of you is not condescension. It is precision.

There is a further advantage, one we often overlook. The person across from you may have seen angles you have not. They may carry experience you lack. They may hold a vantage point that never occurred to you. Genuine listening means you may leave a conversation having revised your own position — not because you were defeated, but because you were enlarged.

The goal of communication is not to win. It is to arrive at the most useful outcome for the situation. And that outcome, I have come to believe, is rarely reached by one person thinking alone.

II Clear Meaning and Contexts

Effective communication requires that the other person understand your words as you intended them — not as their own experience has trained them to read them.

Every person carries a different set of references, shaped by age, background, culture, and history. Two people can use the same word and mean entirely different things by it.

Consider the word stress. Near the end of my master's degree, I told a friend how frustrated I felt about my thesis and how stressed I was. He answered with genuine empathy — he got stressed too, especially near the end of a degree. But stress is not a single, uniform experience. It runs along a spectrum, from eustress (the kind that sharpens focus) to the kind that can end in depression.

Because everyone around me seemed to be experiencing stress and coping with it, I minimized what I was going through. Eventually I reached a point where my voice was shaking and my breathing had gone shallow. By then, I had already missed the window to ask for help.

My friend's empathy was real. The shared context was not. The person speaking assumes their meaning has been understood; the person listening assumes their own experience is an adequate measure. Now, when someone tells me they are stressed or anxious, I listen for how it actually affects them.

The same principle extends beyond emotional language. Words like success, respect, and commitment mean different things depending on culture, experience, and stage of life. It is better to clarify than to assume that your sense of commitment is also theirs. Before you communicate something, ask yourself: what does this word mean in practical terms, for this person, right now?

III Asking Questions

The stress example above shows the cost of assumed understanding. There is a remedy: ask, rather than assume.

During one of the hardest stretches of my life, a close friend showed up for me by simply listening. To me, that was the clearest possible expression of care. So naturally, I offered her the same. One day, she brought up how I had missed her birthday while she remembered mine, and that she felt neglected. I had missed her birthday — but birthdays had never held weight for me, not even my own. I was embarrassed. I had wanted her to feel cared for. I had simply done it in my language, not hers.

I apologized, and then I began a practice I now treat as basic to any relationship I value: I ask. How do you feel cared for? How do you show care to others? The answers still surprise me. We are quick to judge people for not caring enough, when the gap lies not in their effort but in what effort looks like to us. What looks effortless to us may have cost them real courage.

IV Empathy — Including Toward Yourself

Empathy is usually understood as the ability to place yourself in someone else's position. Communication needs that, but it also needs the inward version: the willingness to extend the same understanding to yourself, and to say what bothers you before it hardens into resentment. Not only for your own sake, but for the relationship's — professional or personal alike.

Once, working in a shared office, a colleague asked me to mute my notification sounds. I was genuinely surprised, since I kept my notifications off. The noise, I suggested, must be coming from the next room. Out of courtesy, he let it go and went back to work. Minutes later, I clicked something on my laptop, and the sound played again. "That's it," he said at once. I was mortified: I had been so absorbed in what I was doing that I never noticed one of my own clicks was disturbing him. I apologized and fixed it on the spot.

What disturbs other people is often not a failure of empathy but a failure of awareness. We simply do not know that some small thing grates on them until they tell us. That is why staying silent out of politeness is not generosity. It is a quiet accumulation of frustration that could have been avoided.

Many situations that come to feel toxic involve no toxic people. They are the product of small silences. So I have made a practice of inviting the people I live or work alongside to tell me when something bothers them. What costs them real courage to endure may take me only a moment to fix. The reverse holds too: just because nothing has been said does not mean all is well.

— Closing

Above, I have laid out some common principles for reducing the gap in exchanging information and becoming better informed. The goal of this blog was never to make you a master of communication. It was to make you pause the next time you catch yourself thinking: this is too obvious to explain, they should already know this, I assumed you understood. Those phrases are where avoidable difficulty quietly begins.

Communication cannot be fully mastered. There will always be situations that are new for some and routine for others — words that carry weight for one person and nothing for another. That gap never fully closes. But it can be narrowed, consistently and deliberately, simply by choosing to transfer understanding rather than assume it already exists.

There are rigorous books on this subject, and you should read them. But before the books, before the frameworks: sit with the possibility that many of the frustrations you carry — in your relationships, your work, your own sense of being understood — are not inevitable. They are, in large part, a communication problem. And communication problems, unlike many others, are entirely solvable — if you decide they are worth solving.

A little more shared understanding.
A little less unnecessary suffering.
